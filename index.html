5 Product Feedbacks

The Teardown

Each feedback follows the structure: (a) what I observed in the actual product, (b) the real user or business pain, (c) specific, actionable solution.

01
Financial wallet fully accessible with zero identity verification
Compliance Trust & Safety UX
Critical
Observed
I downloaded the app, selected language, gave location permission, and landed directly on the Explore page — no phone number, no name, no OTP, no email, nothing. When I navigated to the Wallet tab, I found a fully functional wallet interface showing Total Balance, Locked Amount, Unlocked Amount, a Withdraw button, a Send button, and an Add Money form with quick-add presets (₹100, ₹200, ₹500, ₹1000) and a "Proceed to Pay" CTA. I also accessed Payout Methods, which showed a full bank account details form (Account Number + IFSC) — again, with no login gate. The Profile page showed a "Know Your Customer" banner prominently — but KYC is never enforced anywhere in the flow.

📍 Wallet tab · Payout Methods screen · Profile KYC banner
Problem
This is a direct conflict with RBI's Prepaid Payment Instrument (PPI) guidelines. Under RBI Master Directions on PPIs (2017, updated 2021), even "small PPIs" require minimum KYC — at least mobile number verification and self-declaration — before any loading or transaction. Showing a withdrawal-enabled wallet and a bank account linking form to an anonymous, unverified user is both a regulatory compliance risk and a trust-destroying product decision. For a fintech app, trust IS the product. When users see financial tools wide open with no verification, their first instinct isn't relief — it's suspicion. Additionally, the KYC banner on the Profile page creates what designers call "compliance theatre" — the appearance of security without the enforcement. This is the worst of both worlds: it adds visual clutter without adding actual protection.
Ship instead
Gate the entire Wallet tab and Payout Methods behind phone number verification with OTP — the moment a user taps either, trigger a lightweight login sheet. This is a single engineering change with massive compliance and trust upside. Separately, replace the decorative KYC banner with an actionable KYC progress bar ("Complete KYC to unlock withdrawals") that only disappears once the user is verified. Add a tooltip on the Locked/Unlocked distinction — "Locked funds are held during active subscriptions and released within 24h of confirmation" — since this is currently invisible and causes most refund complaints on Play Store.
02
"Make Public" toggle is ON by default — a dark pattern that exposes users without consent
UX Trust & Safety
High
Observed
When I tapped "Add Subscriptions" from the Home screen and selected Netflix Standard Plan, a modal appeared to track my subscription. The modal contains a toggle labelled "Make Public" — with helper text: "Making your subscription public allows others to see and join your group." This toggle is turned ON by default. A user who fills in their plan details and taps "Add to Track" without noticing the toggle has unknowingly listed their subscription publicly for strangers to join — without ever intending to become a group admin.

📍 Home → Add Subscriptions → Netflix modal → Make Public toggle
Problem
This is a textbook dark pattern — specifically "privacy zuckering," where a product's default settings benefit the platform (more listings = more supply = more revenue) at the expense of user privacy and intent. A user tapping "Add to Track" is expressing a personal intent: I want to manage my subscription. They are not necessarily expressing a social intent: I want to share this with strangers. By defaulting to public, Subspace conflates these two very different user jobs. This also has downstream consequences — an accidental public listing means the user may get join requests from strangers, creating confusion and potential trust issues. India's DPDP Act (2023) requires explicit, informed consent for sharing personal data. A pre-ticked "share publicly" toggle likely does not satisfy this standard.
Ship instead
Default "Make Public" to OFF. This is a one-line code change. After the subscription is added, show a contextual nudge: "Your Netflix is tracked privately. Want to earn money by sharing a slot with others? → Make it public." This turns a dark pattern into a growth mechanic — users who opt in are genuinely motivated admins, not accidental ones. This also improves supply quality on the marketplace, since willing admins are better admins. The nudge can be A/B tested for conversion without compromising user trust.
03
Phone number silently embedded in AI Mailbox email address — a hidden privacy breach
Features Trust & Safety Compliance
Critical
Observed
In the Account section under "AI-powered Mailbox," the app has automatically created a Subspace email address for me. The generated address is 7587033588rylo.club — literally my phone number prepended to a domain. This email was created silently, without any prompt, explanation, or consent screen during onboarding. The feature is described as "smart email filtering and management" that blocks spam and delivers only transactional emails to your group chat. There is no option shown to change, customize, or delete this address on the screen.

📍 Account → AI-powered Mailbox
Problem
Using a user's phone number as a publicly visible email address username is a severe privacy violation. Any person or service that receives an email from 7587033588rylo.club immediately knows the sender's mobile number — a piece of personally identifiable information the user may have shared only for OTP verification. This defeats the purpose of an email alias entirely. Beyond the individual privacy harm, this likely violates the DPDP Act (2023), which requires informed consent before processing or repurposing personal data. The user never consented to having their phone number used as a public email identifier. The feature also has zero discoverability — most users will never find the AI Mailbox section and won't know this address was created on their behalf.
Ship instead
Generate email aliases using a random username format (e.g., tanisha.x7k2@subspace.mail) that contains no PII. Give users the ability to customize their alias during setup with a clear consent screen explaining: "Subspace will create a private email alias for your group communications. Your phone number will not be visible." Add an opt-in flow for AI Mailbox rather than silent auto-creation — users who understand the feature will use it better. This turns a privacy liability into a genuine product differentiator: a smart, private inbox for subscription communications.
04
No trust signals on group listings — the root cause of Subspace's refund and kick-out crisis
Features UX Trust & Safety
High
Observed
The Shared Subscriptions section on the Explore page lists groups with only three data points visible on each card: service name (truncated — e.g., "Netflix Standard Plan"), per-device price, and group count (e.g., "149+ groups"). There is no admin rating, no verified badge, no response time, no kick history, no slot availability, no review count visible at listing level. A user choosing between 149+ Netflix Premium groups has zero quality signal — they are choosing blind. Every card looks identical regardless of admin quality.

📍 Explore → Shared Subscriptions grid
Play Store reviews confirm the downstream damage: "Admin removed me from the group because I requested an OTP twice." "Subscription hidden without explanation, support not responding." "Once you pay, you lose your money if the subscriber logs you out." These are not edge cases — they reflect a systemic trust gap.
Problem
Subspace's core product requires users to pay real money to a stranger and trust that stranger to maintain access, share OTPs, and not kick them arbitrarily. This is an inherently high-trust transaction. Yet the listing surface provides no trust infrastructure whatsoever — no reputation system, no accountability mechanism, no dispute signal. The result is a power asymmetry: admins hold all the cards (credentials, group control, refund decisions) while members have no recourse. This isn't just a UX problem — it's the primary driver of the 3.6★ rating, the negative reviews, and the refund requests. Fixing the listing surface addresses the symptom; fixing the underlying admin accountability model addresses the cause.
Ship instead
Introduce an Admin Trust Score — a composite metric visible on every listing card, computed from: response time to OTP requests, kick rate (% of members removed vs. completed tenure), review score from past members, and tenure on platform. Surface this as a simple badge system: "Verified Admin · 4.8★ · 98% completion rate." Add a "Subspace Guaranteed" tier for top-performing admins where Subspace escrows payment and mediates disputes — this directly competes with the trust gap and creates a premium supply tier. Also fix card truncation: "Netflix Standard Plan · 1 Month" should never be cut to "Netflix Standard Plan ·..." — this is a CSS overflow fix that takes an hour.
05
The app tries to serve three incompatible ICPs in one surface — and serves none of them well
GTM & ICPs Features UX
High
Observed
The Home screen of the app surfaces four feature cards to a first-time user: "Add Subscriptions," "Bill Payments," "Events (Bid to earn exclusive rewards)," and "Sell Products — Manage & list your vendor products." Tapping "Sell Products" reveals a full Vendor Dashboard ("Sell Products. Grow Revenue.") with product listing tools, pricing controls, and live status tracking — accessible without any login. The Blogs section contains a category called "Superflow" (a separate B2B product by the same founder) with no explanation for consumer users. Meanwhile, the Explore page — the most compelling screen in the app — is buried as the second navigation tab, not the default landing screen.

📍 Home → Sell Products → Vendor Dashboard · Blogs → Superflow category · Navigation tab order
Problem
Subspace is simultaneously targeting three distinct ICPs: (1) consumers who want to save on subscriptions, (2) subscription group admins who want to earn by sharing, and (3) B2B vendors who want to list products. These are fundamentally different users with different intents, different trust requirements, and different activation flows. Mixing all three into a single undifferentiated Home screen creates what product teams call "feature soup" — the user cannot quickly understand what the app is for or what to do first. A college student looking to save on Netflix has zero context for a Vendor Dashboard. A vendor has zero context for subscription sharing. This ICP confusion also manifests in the navigation: the Explore page (best for consumers) is Tab 2, while Home (best for nobody) is Tab 1. The Refer & Earn page also contains a live bug — it displays "50000% off up to ₹50" instead of the intended "50% off" — a copywriting error on the app's primary viral growth mechanic.

📍 Account → Refer & Earn → "50000% off" live copy error
Ship instead
Introduce a lightweight role-selection during onboarding — two paths: "I want to save on subscriptions" (consumer) and "I want to share/sell" (admin/vendor). This single screen dramatically simplifies the experience for both segments. For consumers: default landing tab to Explore, remove Vendor Dashboard from Home, add a simple savings dashboard. For admins and vendors: surface earning tools, admin analytics, and group management in a dedicated experience (or a separate lightweight app, à la Swiggy vs. Swiggy for Business). Fix the Refer & Earn copy bug immediately — "50000% off" is live on a viral-sharing surface and is destroying referral credibility every day it remains.

The Bigger Picture

Subspace has built something genuinely rare — a bootstrapped, profitable marketplace with real network effects and ₹36.5Cr ARR. The core insight (subscription sharing as a savings mechanism) is strong and India-native. The moats are real: data flywheel, first-mover in India's subscription marketplace, and a founder willing to go all-in.


But the product is at an inflection point. The 3.6★ Play Store rating, the trust gap between admins and members, the compliance exposure, and the ICP confusion are not minor polish issues — they are structural risks that compound as the platform scales. A bad experience with one admin poisons the well for the entire marketplace. A compliance incident with RBI stops everything.


The 5 feedbacks above are ordered by impact on trust and retention — the two things that will determine whether Subspace becomes India's subscription OS or a cautionary tale. Each one is a Day-1 priority, not a roadmap item.
